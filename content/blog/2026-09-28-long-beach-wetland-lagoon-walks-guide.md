Our [first trail guide](/blog/2026-08-04-best-hikes-bluff-beach-walking-paths-long-beach) covered the obvious climbs and coastal paths: Signal Hill, the Bluff Park loop, the beach bike path, the Naples canals. This one is about the quieter water. Long Beach sits on what used to be a single huge estuary. Most of it was dredged, filled, or paved, but restored pieces survive, and they make some of the calmest walking in the city.

Fall is the time to go. Southern California sits on the Pacific Flyway, so migrating birds are arriving, the summer beach crowds are gone, and October mornings are usually mild and dry. Every walk below is flat, most are free, and none needs more than good shoes and some patience.

## Colorado Lagoon: Long Beach's Tidal Lagoon, Belmont Heights & Alamitos Heights

**Where:** 5119 E Colorado St
**Distance:** About 1.1 miles around, roughly 30 feet of elevation gain
**Cost:** Free to walk; the lot is paid parking

Colorado Lagoon is an 18-acre saltwater tidal lagoon, once part of the Los Cerritos Wetlands and still connected to Alamitos Bay. Earlier restoration phases added a decomposed-granite walking trail on the north shore, native plantings, a vegetated bioswale, and pedestrian bridge upgrades. On the loop you'll find a boardwalk crossing, a swimming beach, and good birding over open water.

**Honest note before you go:** the city's Colorado Lagoon Open Channel Project is still under construction. It will connect the lagoon to Marine Stadium through an open channel. Parts of Colorado Street and Eliot Street have been closed while the work continues, and the project has run past its original schedule. The city's project page lists a temporary pedestrian path, and walking the full loop means using some neighborhood sidewalks. Follow the detour signs, and check the Public Works project page before you drive over.

## Jack Dunster Marine Biological Reserve: Marine Stadium, East Long Beach

**Where:** End of Boathouse Lane, beside the Pete Archer Rowing Center
**Distance:** About 0.4 miles of meandering path
**Cost:** Free

This is the smallest walk on the list, and maybe the most surprising. The city built the 2.7-acre reserve on the Los Cerritos Channel: 1.5 acres of land and 1.2 acres of shallow tidal water. Gangways lead to two floating observation platforms and a floating dock, so you can stand over the marsh itself. The plantings recreate coastal sage scrub, tidal marsh, mudflat, and rocky intertidal habitat. Leashed dogs are welcome.

Make it a longer outing by walking the banks of Marine Stadium next door. It was the rowing venue for the 1932 Olympics, and rowing crews and paddlers still train there on most mornings.

## Sims' Pond Biological Reserve: Alamitos Heights

**Where:** Loynes Drive and Pacific Coast Highway
**Access:** Viewing from the sidewalk only

Sims' Pond is a 6-acre freshwater marsh and seasonal pond, and it's included here with a clear caveat: it is **closed to the general public**. The Los Cerritos Wetlands Stewards handle tours and upkeep, and you can see into the pond from the sidewalks on PCH and Loynes. Why it's still worth a detour: ducks and egrets use it as a rest stop along the Pacific Flyway. It fits easily into a walk that also takes in Jack Dunster and Marine Stadium.

## Los Cerritos Wetlands: Guided Walks and Kayak Tours on the East Side

**Where:** Meeting points in Seal Beach (1st St & PCH or Gum Grove Park) and at Mother's Beach, 5733 Appian Way, Naples
**Cost:** Nature walks by RSVP; kayak tours ask a $40 donation

Most of the restored Los Cerritos Wetlands sits behind locked gates, which is why a guided walk is worth it. The **Los Cerritos Wetlands Land Trust** runs a monthly nature walk at 8 a.m. on the first Saturday. Tours run about two hours, and you have to stay for the whole tour because the route is inside restricted areas. Close-toed shoes are required. Past routes have covered Zedler Marsh, Marketplace Marsh, and the San Gabriel River levee, where sea turtles are often spotted.

To see it from the water, the Land Trust's **kayak tours** with LA River Expeditions paddle into Steamshovel Slough, a part of the wetlands you can only reach by kayak. Paddlers need to be 12 or older, able to swim, and fit enough for about two hours of paddling. And once a month, the **Aquarium of the Pacific** runs a free habitat restoration morning on the wetlands, pulling invasive plants and collecting trash. It's real outdoor work, and families are welcome.

## Dominguez Gap Wetlands: North Long Beach, Along the LA River

**Where:** 286 W Del Amo Blvd; park at David Molina Park
**Distance:** About a 2-mile loop
**Cost:** Free

On the other side of the city, LA County Public Works turned a 37-acre flood-control spreading basin into a year-round wetland in 2008. Urban runoff and LA River flows now pass through the basin, which filters the water and supports habitat on the way. The loop trail goes around the east basin next to the river. Signs explain how it works, and the waterfowl are good. It's tucked behind a neighborhood with no obvious entrance, so use David Molina Park as your starting point. Pair it with the DeForest wetlands trail from our first guide and you have a full North Long Beach river morning.

## How to Walk Wetlands Well

A few habits make these walks better. **Go early:** birds are most active at first light, and the Land Trust tours start at 8 a.m. for that reason. **Check the tide:** Jack Dunster and Colorado Lagoon look very different at high and low tide, and the kayak tour times are set around high water. **Slow down:** a wetland walk isn't a workout the way a Signal Hill climb is. The benefit comes from walking steadily and paying attention for an hour, which is why so many people find these spots restorative. For more green space nearby, see our guide to [Long Beach's hidden parks](/blog/2026-09-11-long-beach-hidden-parks-rainbow-lagoon-macarthur-houghton).
