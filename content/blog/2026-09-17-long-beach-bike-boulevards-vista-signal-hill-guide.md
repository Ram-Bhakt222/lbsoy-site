The Shoreline Pedestrian Bikepath gets all the postcards, but most of Long Beach's day-to-day cycling happens on calmer streets you'd never find by accident: traffic-calmed bike boulevards threading through residential neighborhoods, a restored greenbelt along the LA River, and one genuinely steep climb with the best view in the city at the top. If the beach path is Long Beach's showpiece, these are its everyday network — and they reach neighborhoods the coastal route never touches.

## Daisy/Myrtle Bike Boulevard — Bixby Knolls to Downtown

The **Daisy/Myrtle Bike Boulevard** is the backbone of north-south cycling in Long Beach: a 9.5-mile route running along Daisy Avenue to the south and Myrtle Avenue to the north, connecting **North Long Beach** and **Bixby Knolls** all the way down to Downtown and the shoreline. Completed by the city's Public Works department in December 2018, the boulevard isn't a separated bike path — it's a full traffic-calming retrofit of an ordinary residential street, with new traffic circles, signals, median islands, and sidewalk work at 25 intersections designed to slow car speeds and make crossing safer for people on bikes. That makes it one of the more comfortable ways to ride the length of the city without ever touching a major arterial: start near the Bixby Knolls business district on Atlantic Avenue, and you can ride calm, low-traffic streets nearly the whole way to Downtown.

## Vista Street Bike Boulevard — A Belmont Heights Shortcut

On the east side of the city, the **Vista Street Bike Boulevard** does the same job on a smaller scale. It's a roughly 1.3-mile stretch running west to east through the **Belmont Heights / Los Altos** area, crossing Argonne, Park, Ximeno, Mira Mar, and Orizaba avenues with traffic circles and a signalized crossing at Redondo Avenue. It's one of the city's older bike boulevard projects — built out in the early 2010s — and it remains an easy, low-stress east-west connector for anyone riding between the Traffic Circle area and the bluff-top neighborhoods above Alamitos Beach, without needing to ride Anaheim Street or 7th Street.

## Wrigley Greenbelt — Riding the LA River's West Bank

On the **Westside**, the **Wrigley Greenbelt** runs along LA River flood-control land bordering the neighborhood, part of the same corridor that carries the **LA River Bike Path** from the City of Vernon down to Shoreline Park in Downtown Long Beach — in fact the Wrigley stretch was the first section of that river path ever built. A 2023 renovation added a mile of dedicated walking trail, new picnic tables, drought-tolerant native landscaping, and a dry-streambed stormwater design, making the greenbelt a genuinely pleasant place to warm up or cool down before or after a longer river ride, not just a utility corridor.

## Climbing Signal Hill — The One Real Hill in a Flat City

Nearly everything in Long Beach is flat, which is exactly why **Signal Hill** stands out. The classic approach starts on Pacific Coast Highway, turns up Temple Avenue, and continues onto Skyline Drive for just under a mile of steady climbing to **Hilltop Park** at 2351 Dawson Avenue — the only real elevation workout most Long Beach cyclists ever get. At the top, a paved loop trail along the ridgeline (easy walking, jogging, or casual riding pace) delivers sweeping views of the Port of Long Beach, the harbor's offshore THUMS islands, Downtown LA, and Catalina Island on a clear day. It's short enough to fold into a weekday ride and worth the climb for the view alone.

## Bike Share Is Expanding, and So Is the Group-Ride Scene

The city has been steadily growing **Long Beach Bike Share** past its original footprint, adding hubs in **Bixby Knolls** and along the Daisy/Myrtle corridor as part of a broader push toward roughly 650 bikes across 110-plus stations citywide, with new hubs concentrated in **West** and **North Long Beach** — filling in gaps in neighborhoods the original rollout skipped. If you'd rather ride with people than solo, **Bike Long Beach** (bikelb.com) keeps a running calendar of casual group rides around the city, including its regular "Bikes and Coffee" meetups that loop through bike boulevards like this one at an easy, no-drop pace — a low-pressure way to learn the calm-street network firsthand rather than piecing it together from a map.

## Why the Calm-Street Network Matters for Everyday Health

The beach path is where visitors ride; boulevards like Daisy/Myrtle and Vista Street are where residents actually commute, run errands, and build a daily movement habit. That distinction matters for health outcomes — a bike commute on a traffic-calmed street is exercise that doesn't require carving out gym time, and Long Beach's flat geography means most of the city is within a genuinely comfortable ride of these routes. For anyone rebuilding movement capacity after an injury or managing a chronic condition, a calm, low-stress ride is often a better starting point than a structured workout, and pairs well with more targeted support — a **[yoga therapist](/yoga-therapy)** can help address the mobility and stability limits a bike ride alone won't touch.
