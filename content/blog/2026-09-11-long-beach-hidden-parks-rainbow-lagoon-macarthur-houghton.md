Ask a Long Beach local to name a park and you'll hear El Dorado, Bluff Park, maybe Recreation Park — the anchors we already covered in our [first parks guide](/blog/2026-07-01-best-parks-green-spaces-long-beach). But Long Beach runs more than 170 parks across roughly 3,200 acres, and most of that system sits quietly in neighborhoods that never make the highlight reel. This guide covers seven of them, spanning Downtown to North Long Beach, each with a specific reason to visit rather than just a patch of grass to pass through.

## Rainbow Lagoon Park: A Quiet Loop in Downtown Long Beach

Tucked between Shoreline Village Drive and Linden Avenue, **Rainbow Lagoon Park** (400 E Shoreline Dr) is a 12-acre surprise in the middle of **Downtown Long Beach's** convention-center corridor. The park is landscaped in a traditional Japanese style around a seawater lagoon, connected by two islands and arched "rainbow bridges," with open lawns, walking paths, and a small sandy beach. It's a genuinely calm five-minute walk from the Convention Center and the Aquarium — the kind of decompression stop that works before a meeting or after a long walk along the Shoreline Pedestrian Bikepath. Swan boat rentals operate seasonally on the lagoon itself, and the surrounding lawns host outdoor events throughout the year via the Arts Council for Long Beach.

## Colorado Lagoon: Long Beach's Calmest Open-Water Swim

In **Alamitos Heights**, on the eastern edge of the city, **Colorado Lagoon** (5119 E Colorado St) offers something the ocean can't: a shallow, wave-free swimming lagoon with lifeguards on duty, a sandy beach on both the north and south shores, showers, and restrooms. The renovated inclusive playground next door was built for two age groups — 2 to 5 and 5 to 12 — with slides, swings, climbing walls, and imaginative play features like a general store and a ship's mast. Beyond swimming, the lagoon supports picnicking, wildlife watching, and model boat sailing, and its pathways are walkable and runnable on their own. For anyone who finds open ocean swimming intimidating, this is the on-ramp — flat, protected water within city limits.

## Marina Vista Park: Waterfront Fields in Belmont Heights

**Marina Vista Park** (5355 Eliot St) sits at 18.2 acres in **Belmont Heights**, surrounded by the Spinnaker Cove and Del Ago residential communities, with the beach directly across the street. Two soccer fields, tennis courts, and a softball diamond make it a genuine sports-first park, and it's home turf for the Belmont Shore youth rugby club. A bicycle path lined with palm trees runs through the property, connecting into the broader Belmont Shore path network. The park dates to a 1982 development on land the city purchased back in 1923 as part of the same acquisition that included Colorado Lagoon, Recreation Park, and Marine Stadium — so if you're chasing the city's oldest recreational land, this is part of that same century-old story.

## Los Cerritos Park: A Playground Built to Look Like a Forest

Out in the **Los Cerritos** neighborhood, **Los Cerritos Park** (3750 Del Mar Ave) punches above its 7.24 acres. The standout is a playground designed to blend into its surroundings — play structures shaped like tree trunks, with climbing, swinging, sliding, and a zipline — set inside ample grassy open space used for soccer and casual recreation. Two tennis courts, a basketball court, jogging paths, and hidden benches for a quiet break round it out, and the whole park is wheelchair accessible. It also hosts a summer concert series on its lawns, giving the neighborhood a low-key gathering spot that doubles as everyday exercise ground.

## MacArthur Park: A $10.5 Million Rehab in Wrigley / Cambodia Town

**MacArthur Park** (1321 E Anaheim St), on the border of the **Wrigley** and **Cambodia Town** neighborhoods, is the newest story in this guide. The city completed a full rehabilitation of the park, celebrated with a ribbon-cutting in 2025, and the result is one of the more thoughtfully redesigned public spaces in Long Beach: upgraded basketball courts with new surfacing and lighting, a walking loop built around traditional and calisthenic fitness equipment, shade structures inspired by Southeast Asian thatched-roof architecture, accessible play equipment, and a re-established lawn for community programming. The park is also home to the Homeland Cultural Center, a venue for Long Beach's Cambodian arts and cultural programs — making this as much a cultural landmark as a fitness stop. Current hours run Monday–Friday 2:30–10 p.m., Saturday 10 a.m.–6 p.m., and Sunday 9 a.m.–6 p.m.

## Houghton Park: North Long Beach's Fitness Hub

**Houghton Park** (6301 Myrtle Ave) anchors **North Long Beach** with three baseball diamonds, an overlaid soccer field, a separate soccer field, tennis and basketball courts, and a skate park built in 2004. The park's half-mile walking loop — already noted in our gyms guide as one of the city's free outdoor Fitness Zones, with bilingual signage and QR-code demo videos — makes it one of the more approachable strength-and-cardio combinations in the city, no membership required. The recently expanded community center adds two multipurpose rooms, a teen center, a computer lab, and a weight room, so Houghton functions as a genuine neighborhood recreation hub rather than just a field.

## Drake Park: A Historic Anchor on the Westside

**Drake Park** (951 Maine Ave) is the oldest park in this guide by pedigree. Originally donated by Colonel Charles R. Drake and taken over by the city in 1904 as "Knoll Park," it later took Drake's name and, after a 1972 HUD-funded expansion, grew into the 6.6-acre park it is today — a basketball court, community center, handball/racquetball court, soccer field, tennis and volleyball courts, two playgrounds, and a small skatepark. It's the centerpiece of the **Drake Park/Willmore City Historic Landmark District**, one of Long Beach's first designated historic areas, and sits close to the preserved 1906 Queen Anne-style Bembridge House. For **Westside** residents, it's the rare park that combines real sports infrastructure with a walkable slice of the city's architectural history.

## Building These Into a Routine

None of these seven require a car trip across town if you already live nearby — that's the point. Rotate Rainbow Lagoon into a Downtown lunch break, use Colorado Lagoon for a lower-stakes swim than the open ocean, and treat Houghton's fitness loop or MacArthur's calisthenics stations as a free, equipment-included strength session.
