Long Beach is famously flat, which fools people into thinking there's nowhere to hike here. There is — you just have to know where the city hides its elevation, its dirt, and its water. Between a 105-acre nature preserve in the east, a working oil field with 360-degree views to the north, four and a half car-free miles along the Pacific, and two restored wetlands most locals have never walked, this is one of the best cities in Southern California for putting miles on your legs without getting in a car first.

Walking is also the most underrated thing you can do for your body. It's free, it's low-risk, it doesn't require a membership or a change of clothes, and it's the single easiest habit to actually keep. Here are the routes worth building that habit around, sorted by what you're in the mood for.

## The Dirt Trail: El Dorado Nature Center, East Long Beach

If you want to forget you're in a city, this is the place. The El Dorado Nature Center is a 105-acre wildlife sanctuary inside El Dorado East Regional Park with more than two miles of trails weaving around lakes, streams, grasslands, and forest. There's a quarter-mile paved loop for strollers and mobility needs, a one-mile dirt trail, and a second one-mile dirt spur off the main loop. Expect turtles, rabbits, and more than 100 bird species, plus redwoods and eucalyptus that make the whole thing feel transplanted from somewhere far north of here.

**Details:** Trails open Tuesday–Sunday, 8 a.m.–5 p.m., with no trail entry after 4:30 p.m.; the Visitors Center runs 8:30 a.m.–4 p.m. Closed Mondays. Admission to the Nature Center is free — you only pay to park a car ($6 weekdays, $7 Friday, $8 weekends, $9 holidays, cashless only). Walk or bike in and there's no fee at all.

## The Real Climb: Signal Hill Trail & Hilltop Park

Signal Hill is the only genuine elevation in the Long Beach basin, and the payoff is disproportionate to the effort. The **Signal Hill Trail** at Hilltop Park runs about 1.6 miles with roughly 206 feet of gain — an easy half-hour to an hour — and delivers 360-degree views of the Port of Long Beach, the THUMS islands, the downtown LA skyline, the San Gabriels, and Santa Catalina Island on a clear day. Twenty-five-cent telescopes are mounted at the summit. Go at sunset; everyone else does, and it's still worth it.

For a longer version, start at **Discovery Well Park** and follow Panorama Road northwest — about two miles past outdoor workout stations and interpretive signs about the hill's oil history before you arrive at Hilltop. Free, open 5 a.m.–10 p.m. daily.

## The Quiet Add-On: Panorama Promenade, Signal Hill

Most people top out at Hilltop and turn around, which means they miss the **Panorama Promenade** — a landscaped third-of-a-mile stretch running from the north end of Dawson Avenue to the corner of Panorama Drive and Molino Avenue. It faces the opposite direction from Hilltop, opening up the view north over Long Beach Airport and the basin beyond. At the dead end, take the stairs up to the Unity Monument. It's the best five extra minutes in the city.

## The Sunset Standard: Bluff Park Loop, Alamitos Beach

The **Bluff Park** loop is Long Beach's default evening walk: a little over a mile along the top of the bluff above Alamitos Beach, down a stairway to the beach path, and back up again. Longer variations along the full Bluff Park trail run closer to 3.3 miles. The stairs are the secret ingredient — they turn a flat stroll into something your legs actually notice. This is also where the free daily **Yoga on the Bluff** class meets at Bixby Park Annex, so you can stack a walk and a session into the same hour.

## The Long Flat One: Shoreline Pedestrian Bikepath, Downtown to Belmont Shore

The **Shoreline Pedestrian Bikepath** is the backbone of active Long Beach — roughly 4.3 to 4.5 paved, flat, car-free miles running from the Shoreline Marina and Shoreline Village in Downtown all the way to Belmont Shore. There's no traffic to negotiate and no elevation to manage, which makes it the right pick for anyone rebuilding capacity after an injury, walking with kids, or just wanting to zone out for an hour. Out-and-back from Shoreline Village to Belmont Shore is a solid 9-mile day.

## The Prettiest Loop: Naples Canals, Naples Island

The **Naples Canals Loop** is an easy 2.5-mile route through the island's canal-side walkways and footbridges — usually 30 minutes to an hour, and unquestionably the most charming walk in the city. Extend it to the full **Naples Island loop** and you're looking at about 4.5 miles and a bit over two hours. There's no dirt and no climb here; the appeal is that you'll keep walking longer than you meant to.

## The Ones Nobody Knows: Willow Springs Park & DeForest Wetlands

Two restored wetlands sit in Long Beach's central and north neighborhoods, and almost nobody outside those neighborhoods walks them.

**Willow Springs Park** covers 48.2 acres with a walking loop threading a series of water-capturing bio-swales that feed roughly an acre of restored seasonal wetlands and 10 acres of native habitat, plus a constructed spring that echoes the original artesian spring the city was built around. The wetlands restoration opened in October 2017. Climb to **Longview Point** for a panoramic look back across Long Beach. Trails vary in difficulty, so you can make it gentle or make it work.

**DeForest Park and Wetlands** in North Long Beach has a nature trail at the park's southern end that winds through riparian forest — cottonwoods, coast live oaks, willows — with short spurs down toward wetlands along the LA River levee. The restored section opened June 30, 2018, adding 34 acres of river parkway, freshwater wetlands, wildlife habitat, and interpretive signage.

## How to Actually Use These

Pick one flat route and one hill route, and alternate. Flat mileage on the Shoreline path or the Naples canals builds aerobic base and is easy to repeat daily. Signal Hill or the Bluff Park stairs add the load your hips, knees, and calves need to stay resilient. Two of each per week is a real training program, and it costs nothing.
